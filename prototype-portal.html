01 · The challenge

Three pain points, one root cause

Revuze's product is dense: thousands of products, reviews, tickets and AI-generated insights on one screen. Static Figma prototypes could show what a feature looked like, but not how it behaved with real data. That gap showed up in three places.

User research

Users reacted to click-through images, not to the feature. We could not see how they used filters, long lists or AI answers until after the feature was built.

Client presentations

Sales and product needed to show clients new features before development, to validate them early. Figma frames were hard to present and easy to misread.

Developer handoff

States, edge cases and data behavior lived in written specs. Developers filled the gaps themselves, and corrections came late, when they cost the most.

Under all three sat the design system. It existed only as one very large Figma file, with no Storybook and no coded components. Developers rebuilt components from screenshots, and nothing tied a design to what Angular could actually render.

02 · My role

What I owned, and the limits I worked within

  • Identified the three pain points and proposed the solution.
  • Designed and built the Prototype Portal, and own its repo.
  • Turned the Figma file into a coded design system that AI and developers both use.
  • Set the workflow: how prototypes are added, grouped, reviewed and handed off.

I was the only designer at Revuze, working with product managers and R&D leads.

ConstraintThe product is built in Angular. Anything I designed had to be buildable with its components.
ConstraintNo extra engineering time. The solution had to come from design, not from a dev project.
ConstraintInternal and client-facing at once, so access had to stay limited to company accounts.
03 · The thinking

Why working prototypes, and why a coded system first

I started building features with AI, using Claude connected to Figma through MCP. The first results were fast but inconsistent. Claude invented colors and spacing, and produced components our Angular app did not have. The prototypes looked right and could not be built as drawn.

That made the order of work clear. Before AI could build reliable prototypes, it needed rules. So the design system came first.

OptionUpsideWhy I didn't stop there
Keep the Figma file, add documentationNo new toolsStill a picture of components. AI and developers still guess the behavior.
StorybookIndustry standard for coded componentsNeeds engineering time to set up and maintain, which we didn't have.
Figma prototypes onlyFast, familiarCan't show real data, loading, staging or errors. Same gaps as before.
Coded design system + working prototypesOne source for AI, design and devChosen. Mirrors Angular's limits, so what AI builds is what dev can ship.

The key decision was to encode the restrictions of our system, not only its look. Each component exposes exactly the sizes, hierarchies and states the Angular component supports, and nothing else. When Claude builds a prototype, it can only use what exists.

04 · The solution

A coded design system, and a portal that serves all three needs

The Hubs Design System

The Figma file became a living component site. Every component shows all its states and sizes, and an Angular tab holds the code developers copy into the app. The repo instructs Claude to use only these tokens and components. Repetitive Figma work is scripted too: a small plugin generates new Badge sizes from existing ones.

The Prototype Portal

Every feature is built with Claude on the design system and published to one internal site, behind company sign-in. Each prototype has a stable link and a live preview. Options for the same feature sit together in a folder.

One prototype, three uses

How each pain point was solved

User research

Alerts: testing showed the flow was too complex

Alerts tell a brand when something changes, such as a spike in product defects. We tested the full setup flow with users as a working prototype, with real catalog data and AI pre-filling the settings.

Two things came out of testing. The flow had too many decisions, so I simplified it: AI pre-fills what it can, and the user confirms each answer with a chip. Users also hit edge cases we had not planned for, like adding the same email twice. Each one went into the spec before development started.

Client presentations

QA tickets: client feedback revealed the real problem

I presented the QA ticket flow to a few clients before development. They gave feedback based on their real tickets, which reshaped how ticket data is structured in the product.

They also told us what actually took most of their time: linking their products to the Revuze catalog. That feedback became the next feature.

Developer handoff

Product linking: the review changed the spec before any code

Brands link their catalog to Revuze's, many products to many. Reviewing the prototype with engineering replaced pagination with infinite scroll and merged two views into one table. The handoff doc covers behavior only, records both changes, and tells developers to build against it.

What I learned

AI made prototyping fast, but only rules made it reliable. And a working prototype did more than speed up handoff: it put real behavior in front of users and clients early enough for them to change what we built. The most valuable design work here was not a screen. It was defining what the system allows, so that people and AI build the same thing.
